The Falling Apple and the "Miracle Year" Amidst the Plague



When people think of Isaac Newton, they often picture a great man sitting under a tree, getting hit on the head by a falling apple, and instantly discovering the law of universal gravitation. The story sounds like a random anecdote. However, behind that moment of the falling apple was a solitary journey of an abandoned child, a poor student struggling to get by, and especially an extraordinary creativity awakened during one of the darkest pandemics in human history.

The Lonely, Premature Child and the Mother Who Left


Isaac Newton was born in 1642 in Woolsthorpe, England. He came into the world in the exact year the great scientist Galileo Galilei passed away, sounding like a fateful passing of the scientific torch. Newton was a premature baby, so tiny that people said he could fit inside a quart mug, and no one thought he would survive.

Tragedy struck before he was even born when his father passed away. When Newton was three years old, his mother remarried a wealthy man and left the young boy to be raised by his grandmother. The shock of being abandoned by his mother left a deep psychological scar, turning Newton into a quiet, solitary, and deeply insecure child.

At school, Newton was not an outstanding student. He was frequently bullied by his peers due to his eccentric personality. Instead of playing games, he preferred to hide in a corner to build complex mechanical models like water clocks, kites carrying lanterns that frightened the locals at night, or meticulous sundials. One day, after being beaten by a school bully, Newton resolved to defeat his tormentor through intellect. He rose to the top of his class, proving that his mind was his most powerful weapon.

The Great Plague of London and the Fateful "Quarantine Room"


At age 18, Newton entered the University of Cambridge. Because his family did not provide much financial support, he had to work as a "subsizar"—a poor student who served tea, cleaned rooms, and ate the leftovers of wealthy students in exchange for tuition. Despite this discrimination, Newton silently devoured all the mathematical and philosophical knowledge of his predecessors.

In 1665, just as Newton graduated, a horrific disaster struck England: The Great Plague of London. The deadly disease spread so rapidly that the University of Cambridge was forced to issue an emergency closure. Students were ordered to disperse to the countryside to seek refuge.

Newton returned to his family's old farm in Woolsthorpe. During 18 months of social isolation, while the entire country plunged into fear and death, the 23-year-old Newton found a space of absolute quiet. He had no guiding professor, no university library, just a small room, a few notebooks, and a mind desperate to decode the universe.

Scientific history calls these two years of isolation (1665 - 1666) Newton's Annus Mirabilis (The Miracle Year). It was in this absolute solitude that he laid the foundations for the three greatest achievements of his life:

1. Calculus: When the mathematical tools of his time were insufficient to solve his problems, Newton invented an entirely new branch of mathematics.

2. Optics: He ground his own prisms, drilled a small hole in his window to let sunlight pass through, and proved that white light is actually a blend of the seven colors of the rainbow.

3. The Law of Universal Gravitation: And it was right in his backyard garden that he witnessed an apple fall to the ground.

From a Falling Apple to the Rules of the Stars


Newton did not merely ask, "Why does the apple fall straight to the ground instead of flying up or sideways?". He pushed his thinking millions of miles further than the average person: "If this pulling force can tug the apple down from the tree branch, could it extend all the way to the Moon?".

He realized that the very force that keeps the apple falling to the ground is the exact same force that holds the Moon in orbit around the Earth, and keeps the planets orbiting the Sun. With just a simple observation, Newton used mathematics to bind the entire universe to his three classic laws of motion. When the pandemic passed and Cambridge reopened, the rough diamond Newton stepped into the light. At the age of 26, he became the Lucasian Professor of Mathematics—one of the most prestigious academic titles in the world. His subsequent book, Principia (Mathematical Principles of Natural Philosophy), officially laid the foundation for classical physics for over 200 years.

✨ Deep Lessons from Isaac Newton's Life


• Turn times of crisis into a breakthrough opportunity: The Great Plague was a disaster, but for Newton, it was the most precious time to focus completely. When the outside world is chaotic, keeping your mind anchored to a grand goal can help you create life-changing turning points.

• Never underestimate the ordinary: Millions of people had seen apples fall before Newton, but only he stopped to ask macro-level questions. Genius is sometimes not about finding something new in a faraway place, but about seeing the extraordinary in the most familiar phenomena.

• Perseverance in silence: When asked how he managed to discover the laws of the universe, Newton simply replied: "By always thinking unto them." His success was not a stroke of luck, but the result of countless sleepless nights, pondering to the point of exhaustion in solitude.
